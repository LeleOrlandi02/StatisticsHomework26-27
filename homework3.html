2. Rigorous Theoretical & Statistical Comparison

Analyzing mathematical structures, key spaces, security reductions, and empirical entropy.

3. Statistical Cryptanalysis of the Caesar Cipher

Automated key recovery using the Chi-Squared ($\chi^2$) goodness-of-fit statistic against reference English letter frequencies.

Given a known language with characteristic letter probabilities $f_c$ (for $c \in \{'A', \dots, 'Z'\}$), decrypting a ciphertext of length $N$ with a candidate shift $s \in \{0, \dots, 25\}$ produces observed letter counts $O_c(s)$. The expected count is $E_c = N \cdot f_c$. The Chi-Squared ($\chi^2$) Goodness-of-Fit statistic is formulated as:

$$\chi^2(s) = \sum_{c='A'}^{'Z'} \frac{\left(O_c(s) - E_c\right)^2}{E_c}$$

The true key corresponds to the candidate shift $s$ that minimizes the discrepancy between the decrypted text distribution and standard English: $\hat{k} = \arg\min_{s} \chi^2(s)$.

Ciphertext Length Effect & Monte Carlo Simulation

Empirical proof of the Law of Large Numbers (LLN) in statistical cryptanalysis.

Why does statistical frequency analysis fail on short texts? By the Central Limit Theorem, the empirical sample frequency $F_c = O_c / N$ has variance $\text{Var}(F_c) = \frac{p_c(1 - p_c)}{N} \propto O(1/N)$. On ultra-short ciphertexts ($N \le 15$), statistical noise dominates: rare letters appear disproportionately, while frequent letters (like 'E' or 'T') may be absent entirely. Comparative Evaluation of Alternative Cryptanalytic Approaches:

  • 1. Exhaustive Key Search (Brute Force with Dictionary): Because $|\mathcal{K}| = 26$, testing every possible key is computationally trivial ($< 0.1$ ms). However, fully automated evaluation without statistical frequencies requires scoring candidate outputs against an extensive dictionary or natural-language heuristic (e.g., counting valid English word hits).
  • 2. Unigram Peak Matching (Heuristic 'E'): Associating the most frequent letter directly with 'E' is fragile. On texts with $N < 50$, or stylized writing (lipograms, dialogue, proper nouns), letters such as 'A', 'I', or 'O' frequently outnumber 'E', causing peak matching to fail abruptly (accuracy drops below $30\%$ for $N \le 20$).
  • 3. $N$-Gram Scoring (Bigrams & Trigrams): Evaluating bigram likelihood $\sum \log P(c_i c_{i+1})$ leverages second-order Markov transition properties of natural English (e.g., "TH", "HE", "IN", "ER"). As evidenced in our Monte Carlo simulation, bigram scoring achieves $85\%+$ accuracy even on ultra-short ciphertexts ($N = 10-15$) where unigram $\chi^2$ fails due to unrepresentative individual letter distributions!

4. Restricted-Space Toy ECC Cryptanalysis & Hardness Analysis

Exploiting the restricted private-key space ($d \in [1, K_{\max}]$) to break the asymmetric trapdoor and decrypt messages.

In Part 1, the toy scheme employs the genuine secp256k1 curve parameters ($y^2 = x^3 + 7 \pmod p$), but artificially restricts the private key $d$ to a small integer range $d \in [1, K_{\max}]$ (e.g., $K_{\max} = 65,536 = 2^{16}$) to make educational experimentation feasible. Given the public key $Q = d \cdot G$ and a ciphertext tuple $(R, C)$, an attacker can solve the Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem (ECDLP) by testing candidate scalars $d' \in [1, K_{\max}]$ until $d' \cdot G = Q$. Once $d$ is recovered, the adversary computes $S = d \cdot R$ and unmasks the ciphertext.

Algorithmic Differences: Algebraic Discrete-Log Attack vs. Caesar Statistical Attack

Property Statistical Attack (Caesar) Restricted-Space Attack (Toy ECC)
Attack Classification Ciphertext-Only Statistical Attack: Exploits non-uniform frequency distribution of human natural language. Known-Public-Key Algebraic Attack: Directly inverts the one-way group exponentiation $d \mapsto dG$.
Plaintext Dependency Strictly Dependent: Fails completely if the plaintext is high-entropy (random binary, compressed data, foreign language). Completely Independent: Inverts $Q = dG$ without inspecting the plaintext. Works equally on random bits, zeros, or English.
Compromise Scope Recovers the shift $k$ for the specific ciphertext session. Full Identity Compromise: Recovers receiver's private key $d$, allowing retroactive decryption of all past and future messages!
Computational Scaling Constant $O(|\Sigma|) = O(26)$ evaluations regardless of key size. $O(K_{\max})$ for brute force, or $O(\sqrt{K_{\max}})$ via Baby-Step Giant-Step (BSGS).

Why the Discrete-Log Attack Fails on Properly Configured Production ECC

In production configurations (such as Ethereum's secp256k1 or Bitcoin's consensus layer), the private scalar $d$ is drawn uniformly from the entire prime-order group:

$$d \in [1, n - 1], \quad n = 115792089237316195423570985008687907852837564279074904382605163141518161494337 \approx 2^{256}$$

By Shoup's generic group lower bound theorem (1997), any algorithm solving discrete logarithms in a generic group of prime order $n$ without exploiting special algebraic structure requires at least:

$$\Omega(\sqrt{n}) \approx \sqrt{2^{256}} = 2^{128} \approx 3.4028 \times 10^{38} \text{ group operations}$$

Even utilizing the most advanced collision algorithms (Pollard's rho with distinguished points):

  • If an exascale supercomputing cluster performed $10^{18}$ point additions per second (one quintillion ops/sec), completing $2^{128}$ operations would require $1.078 \times 10^{13}$ years — over 780 times the age of the known universe ($\approx 13.8$ billion years).
  • Thermodynamically, under Landauer's Principle ($E \ge k_B T \ln 2$), erasing the bit-states necessary to process $2^{128}$ operations at room temperature requires at least $10^{18}$ Joules, sufficient energy to boil Earth's entire oceans.

Optional Analysis: Restricted Key Space Weaknesses vs. Scheme Design Weaknesses

A rigorous cryptanalytic audit separates flaws stemming from parameter truncation from fundamental architectural deficiencies:

  • 1. Weaknesses Arising Solely from Restricted Key Space:
    • Direct Inversion: Linear accumulation finds $d$ in milliseconds for $K \le 2^{16}$.
    • Baby-Step Giant-Step (BSGS): Time-memory tradeoff reduces operations to $O(\sqrt{K_{\max}}) = 256$ additions with $O(\sqrt{K_{\max}})$ table storage.
    • Pollard's Kangaroo (Lambda) Algorithm: Specifically tailored for discrete logarithms in bounded intervals $[a, b]$, solving $Q = dG$ in $O(\sqrt{b - a})$ operations with negligible $O(1)$ memory.
  • 2. Weaknesses Arising from the Toy Scheme Design Itself:
    • Malleability & Lack of Authentication (CCA Vulnerability): The toy scheme masks bytes without a Message Authentication Code (MAC) or Authenticated Encryption with Associated Data (AEAD). An active adversary (adversary-in-the-middle) can flip ciphertext bits ($C'_i = C_i \oplus \Delta$) to predictably modify the decrypted message without detection.
    • Ephemeral Scalar Reuse ("Two-Time Pad"): If the sender ever encrypts two messages $M_1, M_2$ with the same ephemeral scalar $r$, the shared secret is identical ($S_1 = S_2$). An eavesdropper computes $C_1 \oplus C_2 = M_1 \oplus M_2$, completely eliminating the cryptographic key.
    • Point Validation Failures (Invalid Curve Attacks): If the receiver accepts arbitrary coordinates $(R_x, R_y)$ without verifying $y^2 = x^3 + 7 \pmod p$, an attacker can supply points on weaker twist curves with small group orders, recovering $d \pmod q$ via the Pohlig-Hellman reduction.
    • Industry Solution (ECIES): Modern standards avoid raw stream masking; production systems deploy ECIES (Elliptic Curve Integrated Encryption Scheme), combining ECDH with HKDF-SHA256 and AES-256-GCM or ChaCha20-Poly1305.
